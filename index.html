A chip is a stack of layers, and its heat has to cross every one of them. 42theory solves that stack exactly from the design alone, then works backward from what the design must meet: from a temperature limit to the power each block can still take.

What each
band does

Silicon's band gap is 1.12 eV, so light much shorter than 1.1 µm is absorbed in it and longer light passes through. Inside a chip that gives the three infrared bands three different jobs, and the solver ties each one to the heat.

  1. 01.

    NIR, 0.7 to 1.0 µm. Absorbed within micrometres to a millimetre of silicon: 19 µm at 850 nm. Wherever it is absorbed it becomes heat, and the solver adds that heat there.

  2. 02.

    SWIR, 1.1 to 2.5 µm. Crosses the silicon almost untouched. It is the band silicon photonics uses to carry data on chip, at 1.31 and 1.55 µm, and silicon's refractive index rises by 1.8 × 10⁻⁴ per kelvin, so on a chip that carries light, the temperatures solved here decide how far its optics drift.

  3. 03.

    LWIR, 8 to 14 µm. The warm die emits it. At the hottest point of the back, a black surface would radiate 300 W/m² to 25 °C surroundings, 0.04% of what the coolant takes there, so this model leaves emission out of the cooling and says so.

  4. 04.

    One solver for all three. Absorbed light enters the solve as heat, cell by cell. Here, 1 W of 980 nm light enters the back in a 2 mm spot: 94% is absorbed in the silicon and the rest at the transistors. The hottest point of the chip rises 0.22 K, and heat in still equals heat out to 3 × 10⁻¹⁴.

Solve backward
from a limit

The heat in a chip is linear in its power. So the solver runs once per block, and from those 13 responses it answers backward from a requirement, with no new solve: how far the whole workload can rise, and how many watts each block can add, before any transistor reaches the limit. Each answer carries a 95% interval from 200 more solves with the uncertain inputs drawn from their ranges.

Why it is fast

Layers that are uniform side to side let a cosine transform split the 3-D problem into independent columns: 2,304 small tridiagonal solves, one per lateral mode. Independent solves are what a GPU runs in parallel, and batched tridiagonal solvers are standard GPU library routines.

  1. 01.

    Forward solve: 11 ms on this device, one CPU thread, JavaScript. Every layer, every one of 32,256 cells, steady state.

  2. 02.

    Transient: 0.1 s for 108 implicit time steps from 1 ms to 4 s, the heating shown in the story.

  3. 03.

    A new workload: 0.1 ms once the 13 block responses exist, against 11 ms for a fresh solve. Each response is one forward solve; after that a workload, a limit or a sweep is a sum of 13 fields.

  4. 04.

    Intervals: 0.4 s for 200 solves of the transistor layer, each with its own draw of the uncertain inputs. One such solve takes 2 ms.

  5. 05.

    Checked, not assumed. An independent finite-volume residual of the full 3-D system: 1 × 10⁻¹³.

Perimeter

The solver and our study share one piece of mathematics: a cell complex whose borders carry signs. Perimeter builds one for each picture and gives a vision transformer its regions instead of square patches. The solver builds one for each design. In both, one signed pass over the borders makes the sums exact, and the cost follows the parts, not the pixels.
