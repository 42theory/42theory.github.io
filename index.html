42theory builds solvers for heat and light. Here the problem is a chip: from an infrared image of its back, recover the power of every block inside it, with an interval on each number.

A chip is a stack of layers, and its heat has to cross every one of them. 42theory solves that stack exactly, then runs it backward: from what a camera sees on the surface to what each block underneath is doing.

From surface
to answer

Three steps, the same in every problem we take on: model the physics, measure what can be measured, and solve backward for what cannot, with the uncertainty attached.

What each
band sees

Silicon's band gap is 1.12 eV, so light much shorter than 1.1 µm is absorbed in it and longer light passes through. That turns the infrared into three different instruments.

  1. 01.

    NIR, 0.7 to 1.0 µm. Absorbed within micrometres to millimetres of silicon: 19 µm at 850 nm. It reports on the surface it lands on, not on what lies under the die.

  2. 02.

    SWIR, 1.1 to 2.5 µm. Crosses the silicon. From the backside it images the wiring and bumps under the transistors, which is how flip-chip dies are inspected without opening them.

  3. 03.

    LWIR, 8 to 14 µm. The warm die emits it. A thermal camera reads the backside temperature, and lock-in thermography finds faults by the heat they make.

  4. 04.

    One solver for all three. Absorbed light becomes heat, and heat sets emission. Radiative transfer and heat conduction share one model and one receipt.

Run it
backward

IBM mapped power on a PowerPC processor this way: image the backside through an infrared-transparent heat sink, write the temperature as a sum of each block's own heat field, and solve for the powers. Here it runs live, with intervals and a check that says when not to trust them.

Why it is fast

Layers that are uniform side to side let a cosine transform split the 3-D problem into independent columns: 2,304 small tridiagonal solves, one per lateral mode. Independent solves are what a GPU runs in parallel, and batched tridiagonal solvers are standard GPU library routines.

  1. 01.

    Forward solve: 11 ms on this device, one CPU thread, JavaScript. Every layer, every cell, steady state.

  2. 02.

    Transient: 0.1 s for 108 implicit time steps from 1 ms to 4 s, the heating shown in the hero.

  3. 03.

    Inverse: 6 ms once the 13 block responses exist. Each response is one forward solve.

  4. 04.

    Checked, not assumed. An independent finite-volume residual of the full 3-D system: 1 × 10⁻¹³.

Perimeter

The blocks above are regions, not pixels, and that is the idea of our study. Perimeter replaces the square patches of a vision transformer with the regions of an oriented cell complex. Every border carries a sign. One signed pass returns each region's exact area, centre and spread, so the model sees parts, not pixels.

Request the full study
  1. 01.

    +1.8 points top-1 over plain patches on all three seeds, with 69% of the multiply-adds. Imagewoof at 128 px, CPU pilot.

  2. 02.

    Flat with resolution. 13.0 to 14.4 regions per image from 64 to 1,024 px, where patches grow with the square of the side.

  3. 03.

    Proved. The signed pass recovers exact area, centroid and second moments. Without signs, two different regions can give the same inputs.

  4. 04.

    Not yet shown. A gain from the signs themselves: 0.25 points, inside seed noise. On a CPU the pilot ran at 349 images/s against 704. GPU throughput is measured on our H100 runs, not claimed here.
