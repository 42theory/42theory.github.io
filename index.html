Solve backward
from a limit

Heat is linear in power. So the solver runs once for each of the 14 parts of the GPU's floorplan and once for the memory, and from those 15 responses it answers backward with no new solve: how much of its power the GPU can draw before any transistor or any DRAM die reaches its limit. Change the stack, the bonds, the interface or the cooling; each answer carries a 95% interval from 200 more solves.

Healthcare: finding cancer at the lowest dose

Low-dose CT finds lung cancer early, and CT colonography finds polyps without a scope, but every scan is X-ray dose. The simulator builds virtual patients from tissue attenuation data, scans them with the statistics of the photons, reconstructs the slice and measures how detectable a nodule or polyp is at every dose. Across 200 virtual patients it answers what a screening protocol has to settle: how much dose detection needs, and for whom.

  1. 01.

    Photons are not what limits a solid nodule. Its contrast against air-filled lung is so high that an ideal reader finds it far below today's doses. Radiologists reading a chest phantom with iterative reconstruction found 95% of solid nodules at 0.03 mGy and all of them at 0.28 mGy; the screening limit is 3 mGy.

  2. 02.

    Ground-glass nodules are limited by the anatomy. Their contrast against the lung is a few hundred HU, a third of the vessels around them, so the vessels, not the photons, set the limit: past a point more dose does not help, and d′ levels off at 5.3 to 7.6 across patients, just above the threshold. That is where 3-D reading, better reconstruction and AI readers have to earn their place.

  3. 03.

    The gap is the opportunity. Between the physics floor and the dose used today lies the room that better reconstruction and readers can take. A virtual trial measures it per patient size and lesion before any patient is scanned, the way the FDA's VICTRE trial replicated a clinical trial with 2,986 virtual patients.

Where we compete

Stacking made heat the constraint that decides a chip's design: without mitigation, imec's 3-D memory-on-GPU model peaked at 141.7 °C. Design loops that tie workload, power, packaging and throttling together need the thermal answer at every step, thousands of times, not once at sign-off. That loop is where we compete: the exact answer, on the hardware a team already has, in milliseconds.

Why it is fast

Layers that span the die let a cosine transform along each side split the 3-D problem into independent columns: 2,464 small tridiagonal solves of 35 cells, one per lateral mode. The work grows as N log N in the number of cells, every column is independent, and nothing iterates, so the answer is exact to rounding.

  1. 01.

    Forward solve: 42 ms on this device, one CPU thread, JavaScript. Every layer, every one of 86,240 cells, steady state.

  2. 02.

    Transient: 0.3 s for 120 implicit time steps from 1 ms to 30 s, the heating shown above.

  3. 03.

    A new workload: 1 ms once the 15 responses exist, against 42 ms for a fresh solve. After that a workload, a limit or a sweep is a sum of fields.

  4. 04.

    Intervals: 2 s for 200 solves of the watched layers, each with its own draw of the uncertain inputs. A design sweep of 10,000 stack-ups is minutes on a laptop.

  5. 05.

    Checked, not assumed. An independent finite-volume residual of the full 3-D system: 1 × 10⁻¹³.

Perimeter

The solver and our study share one piece of mathematics: a cell complex whose borders carry signs. Perimeter builds one for each picture and gives a vision transformer its regions instead of square patches. The solver builds one for each floorplan. In both, one signed pass over the borders makes the sums exact, and the cost follows the parts, not the pixels.
